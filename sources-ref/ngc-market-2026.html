Jim Bisognani: My 15th Annual NGC Year in Review – Part 3

Posted on 1/29/2026

Jim asks other experts to weigh in on what they think the future holds for the numismatic market and hobby.

Well, my fellow coindexters, it has been one wild ride since my last report. On the weather front, I know it's winter, but gosh — we got dumped on with nearly 30 inches of snow this past weekend, courtesy of the mammoth storm that swept through much of the US!

On another front, the metals market is also causing shockwaves. Since my last report — just two weeks ago, mind you — gold has surged ahead by nearly $1,000 more, peaking out at over $5,500 per ounce. Meanwhile, silver has broken well into the triple digits, claiming nearly $120 per ounce! I mean, really, who could've predicted this? Exactly one year ago, gold spot was $2,740 per ounce and silver was $31.40 per ounce. I mean, really, let's see a show of hands of who had these numbers.

I guess I can't really complain. I did pick the Patriots and Seahawks to be playing in the Super Bowl LX back in November... but that's another topic.

With so much metal flowing to refineries, they are backed up to the gills trying to meet demand. Based on the current market and refinery back log, 90% junk US silver is being sold $8 below spot price per ounce. That's truly an amazing opportunity, in my opinion. How will this ultimately impact the US and world coin market? It's anyone's guess. My feelings are that rare coins and collectible coins will do extremely well in this market.

Well, I guess it's time to rewind to those more peaceful days (two weeks ago) when I polled my fellow coindexters for my 15th annual NGC Year in Review. Wow, it does seem a bit like ancient history. Now, without further ado, I proudly present the final installment of my 15th Annual Market Review, Part 3.

Do you have any concerns about the present state of the hobby and the coin business?

Jim Stoutjesdyk - Senior Vice President and Partner of Heritage Auctions

The thing that probably concerns me the most is the proliferation of really high-quality counterfeits that I'm seeing. I suspect these are mostly coming out of China. It used to be easy to spot things like a counterfeit Bust Dollar with a reeded edge that could literally be condemned as fake from across the room. But now, I'm seeing really high-quality counterfeits that are then artificially circulated, making them so good that they would fool the majority of coin dealers. Probably the craziest thing I've seen recently is a counterfeit GSA Dollar. The coin was counterfeit, the GSA case was counterfeit, the box was counterfeit, and even the card was counterfeit — sheesh! Scary.

Jeff Garrett – Founder of Mid-American Rare Coin Galleries, Inc.

One of the biggest concerns for the hobby is the high price points for anything bullion related. Double Eagles now melt for over $4,000 and there is no numismatic premium attached to even high-grade examples. The same thing is now happening to Silver Dollars. Many Morgan and Peace Dollars are now selling for below melt value. The US Mint is also having trouble with higher bullion prices. Their DC Comics coins are being met with little interest, mostly because of the outrageous price points.

This 1904-S $20 graded NGC MS 61 and 1921 Morgan Dollar graded NGC MS 62 each sold for very close to melt value this week on eBay. Check out NGC's Coin Melt Values resource here.
Click images to enlarge.

Jeff Kierstead – Owner of MintProducts Auctions

I'm somewhat concerned that the gold and silver prices have priced some collectors out of the market. That said, I think that there are enough other interesting series that budget-minded collectors can concentrate on.

Kevin Lipton - Owner of Kevin Lipton Rare Coins

Our industry has never been more diverse, from the newest buyers to collectors with eight-figure budgets.

John Brush – President and Owner of David Lawrence Rare Coins

The industry / hobby is in an interesting place right now. The tremendous amounts of money entering the hobby inevitably brings about different problems along the way. The addition of private equity companies investing in the largest entities within the hobby bring people who aren't really interested in the growth or future of the hobby. With folks more interested in the bottom line of such companies instead of the future of the hobby, it becomes very concerning and can create many issues for the present and the future.

Brian Hodge – Partner of Lee Minshull Rare Coins (LMRC)

Certainly. Today's interest rates make it harder to carry inventory for a dealer unless you have all the cash in the world to do it. And that is often true for the buyer as well. Some people can place their money in the market better than they can in coins right now. It's a hobby at the end of the day.

Chris B. – Ancients Collector from New Jersey

The success of the coin market rests largely in the hands of the third-party grading services, like NGC and PCGS. Should either close down, it would be a major catastrophe for the rare coin market.

James Sibley – Collector from Texas

A couple: first, how high is up? At what point does the average collector begin to lose interest in the hobby due to rapidly escalating prices? Second, will there be ample buyers around when I (or my estate) goes to sell my collection? It seems that many of our younger collectors are attracted more to modern coins (Wonder Woman, anyone?) than the ones that I collect.

What are the best options for those on an unlimited budget?

Jeff Garrett

If a collector truly has an unlimited budget, they should buy every coin available in my book, 100 Greatest U.S. Coins. These classics will always be in demand.

Kevin Lipton

Buy the "trophy coins," of course!

John Brush

I've always said that affordable type coins under $2,000 are great values. I think that the marketplace has finally caught up with that, and the values on certified coins in that price threshold have increased dramatically due to the number of new collectors that have found the hobby through various internet sales channels. I would stay away from classic gold coins right now because the entry level is so high, but if I wanted to buy a single coin under $5,000 right now, I'd buy an MS 66 Saint-Gaudens. The premiums on these have plummeted as the price of gold has skyrocketed, so I think they are good investments.

Jeff Kierstead

With an unlimited budget, I would buy a 1913 Liberty Nickel the next time one came up. Especially if it was the one from Hawaii 5-O that I saw on TV when I was a kid!

Dan Goevert – Rare Coins 101

Unlimited? If I had no boundaries, I'd track down the current owner of the 1794 SP 66 Flowing Hair Silver Dollar — which is believed to be the very first Silver Dollar produced by the US — and present them with an offer that couldn't be refused. (Not in a Don Corleone sense; I'm talking about a significant amount of money, nothing more. Just making that clear, haha!) This remarkable coin last changed hands in 2013 (that we know of), selling for over $10 million. One wonders how much it would take to pry it away from the current owner.

Jim Stoutjesdyk

I guess the most important advice I can give is to pay attention to quality and originality. If a client gave me millions of dollars and told me to go out and buy him some coins, I could easily and quickly do that. But to find coins that I think are solid for the grade, super original, are really attractive and meet my personal standards would take some time. I have a client who has meticulously picked out incredibly attractive and original coins over the years, buying maybe one after looking at dozens of the same coin, and he is going to be handsomely rewarded when it comes time to sell them. Quality and originality will always be in demand.

Brian Timmons - Owner of Brian Timmons Rare Coins (BTRC)

If a collector's budget is unlimited, I woudl suggest purchasing finest-known examples of ultra-rarities, particularly 18th and 19th century US gold and silver Type coins, with a focus on key dates in Top Pop. Next would be pre-'33 gold and silver coins in the same condition.

Chris B.

Pick another hobby! Just kidding — I love coins, yet here in the US, world coins are still largely undiscovered, as are ancients.

James Sibley

I think I did pretty well during my working career, but it's becoming harder and harder to land the premier coins when I'm competing with folks who, as a result of getting rich easily and quickly (think tech zillionaires) don't have the same concept of the value of a dollar as I do (I got paid a nickel per home for delivering a week's worth of papers). All that to say, it's harder for me to pay an extraordinary amount of money for a coin with money I worked so hard to earn, versus the tech zillionaires who didn't work nearly as hard. That being said, I do occasionally beat a tech zillionaire at an auction... occasionally.

Are there hidden values in circulated 18th century US type coins?

Jeff Kierstead

Any original, problem-free pieces. I think Choice early copper especially fits the bill.

John Brush

I think that these are great values across the board. I'm not so sure that they are "hidden" anymore as they've gone up in price over the past few years, but if I was a lover of history and wanted to start some place, I think there's zero downside on these kind of pieces when they are of nice quality and eye appeal.

Jim Stoutjesdyk

Collectors always want a US coin with a date from the 1700s. I know I certainly did when I was a kid. I even have a young guy at Heritage who I recently hired show me a beat-up Large Cent dated in the 1790s that he was really proud to add to his collection. So I guess the value in circulated 18th century US type coins isn't hidden — it's in plain sight. Any circulated US coin with a date from the 1700s will always be in demand and always appreciate in value as more people want them.

Also, don't overlook US colonial coins. I've had a love for colonial coins since I was a kid and still get excited when I see something like a "Gem" circulated New Jersey Copper in Very Fine condition with pristine surfaces and the perfect brown color.

Jeff Garrett

I love 1794-1803 Bust Dollars. I like to call these the "Silver Dollars of the Founding Fathers." They were made when the country was being formed and are incredibly affordable for what they are.

Kevin Lipton

1796 and 1797 Half Dollars, 100%. All the early gold coins are also still reasonable in nice circulated grades.

Brian Timmons

Yes, all over the place, but finding the coins in uncleaned condition is the hard part!

Brian Hodge

It's a limited run but any early copper or silver still seems to be a good value, or even early gold pieces.

Chris B.

I am not an expert, but I would go for the lowest mintage and the best quality you can afford.

James Sibley

Jim, did you mean the 18th century and 19th century, since there are so few coins available in the former time period? Answering as if you meant to include the 19th, "Yes and no." While a PF 66 Three-Cent Nickel — with fewer than 1,000 coins minted — is incredibly cheap when compared to a 1909-S VDB Cent or a 1916-D Mercury Dime in a similar grade, who will be around to take that coin off of my hands when it's time to sell? I'm not sure that, in the long run, I’m not throwing money into a hole in the ground by collecting classic US Type. Will there be enough similar collectors when my collection is sold in, say, 15 years? Just playing Devil's Advocate here — regardless of whether my wife gets any money for my coins when I'm gone, I'm not going to stop collecting. That's my story and I'm sticking to it!

NGC-graded ancient coins are historically important and are making quite the robust splash in the market. This is especially true for NGC Ancients coins graded MS Choice, Gem and ★, and 5/5 for Strike and 5/5 for Surface. What are your feelings about this segment of the market?

Jim Stoutjesdyk

I was initially opposed to slabbing ancient coins, as were many collectors who felt that it was sacrilegious to entomb these miniature works of art into a piece of plastic and attempt to put them into some sort of category when they all have their own unique character and personality. However, over the years I've come to see the light and now believe that ancient coins, particularly those elusive multiple 5/5 graded coins, need to be slabbed to protect them from being damaged, preserve them for future generations and to guarantee their authenticity.

I represented a collector in 2000 in a European auction and helped them purchase a Gem Gaius (Caligula) Gold Aureus in NGC MS★, with 5/5 Strike and 5/5 Surface for a price that ended up being around $350,000 converted to USD. I sold it for him in our January 2025 NYINC auction and it realized $600,000. Needless to say, he is definitely a believer in the value of slabbed high-grade ancient coins!

Click images to enlarge.

Jeff Garrett

One of my companies is Mid-American Ancients. We love ancient coins and find them to be of great value in all grades. The coins are hard to find and the market for them is global. Very few ancient coins receive Choice, Gem or Star designations. The coins are not only rare, but incredibly beautiful in those grades. The coins are miniature pieces of art from the ancient world and trade as such.

Bob Green – Owner of Park Avenue Numismatics

We're not very active in the ancients market, but see a lot of activity from a small number of dealers. Old doesn't always mean rare, but on the other hand, owning a Twelve Caesars set in MS 5/5 could cost over a million dollars!

Kevin Lipton

I love ancient coins. They sell extremely well on our television shows. The larger and more attractive coins work best, especially if they come with a great historical story.

John Brush

I think that this is very much one of the next places of growth in the hobby. In many ways it is still a developing and somewhat untapped market. There are a number of challenges in this category that I think will have to work themselves out over time, but as those situations solve themselves, I think that the stories, the history and the art of these pieces will certainly prove to be an exciting piece of the hobby.

The challenges at this time are (in no particular order):

  • the lack of a large supply of coins on the market
  • the turnaround times that it takes to get these coins graded
  • the difficulty in determining authenticity of these ancient coins
  • the lack of price discovery that is available to collectors
  • the hesitation from those who have been collecting these for years to grasp the need for certification
  • the authenticity question of so many of these pieces. The lack of clarity in authenticity guarantees that these coins will keep many uninformed "investors" out of this market.

Brian Timmons

This market is quite interesting and I believe coins in those grades will do quite well, long-term price wise.

Jeff Kierstead

I think these are being promoted because they are promotable. They are usually not particularly rare, and they are not on the Greysheet or in price guides, so you can ask whatever the market will bear. It's hard not to find them interesting and appreciate the quality on such an old coin, though.

Brian Hodge

Hands down, without question, the coins of the future. Who doesn't want a 2,000+-year-old coin that is nearly perfect (in some instances)? I see this part of the market exploding exponentially in the next several years.

Chris B.

I only hope that the coins encapsulated are real/original. NGC rules the ancients market, and should NGC ever stop supporting ancients, this segment of the coin market will stagnate and perhaps even lose value and liquidity.

In your opinion, what makes (or doesn't make) modern low-mintage coin collecting a viable component of the numismatic hobby?

Jim Stoutjesdyk

Collecting low-mintage modern coins can be a tricky thing since so many of them are overhyped and overpromoted by telemarketers. This is especially true for the more mainstream issues, like the gold and silver American Eagle series. I would be especially cautious about buying PF 70 and MS 70 coins for exorbitant prices, since over time there will probably be more and more of them graded. However, there are still many issues that have miniscule mintages, like Proof Platinum Eagles and the half-ounce Gold $10 First Spouse series that are worth exploring. Do your homework and study the mintages of the multitude of modern coins that exist, and I'm sure you can find some hidden bargains.

Jeff Garrett

Modern coins with low mintages are often coins that were overlooked when they were produced. The Proof 1995-W Silver Eagle is a great example. The coins were only recognized as very desirable later. I'm a bit uneasy of the US Mint's practice of striking low-mintage coins and selling them at auction. I prefer accidental rarities with a good story.

Bob Green

Every series has its fair share of key dates and low-mintage coins. Moderns are no exception. When we saw a 1995-W American Silver Eagle PF 70 sell for $80,000, we were amazed! But today, they are under $20,000, so consider that many have not been graded yet and, in time, will be. The very first graded and sold 2014 JFK Gold Half Dollar was reportedly sold for $100,000. It makes sense for sensational headlines and that attracts interest from the public. Scores of new buyers try to get in on the next "hot" new issue touted on Reddit. Some are winners, and some are not.

Kevin Lipton

Modern coins are beloved by new collectors. There is no greater introduction to collecting than a complete set of modern Silver Eagles in Mint State or Proof.

John Brush

Low mintage doesn't mean that it's unavailable. Unfortunately, a low mintage of 200+ coins means that there are indeed at least 200 available and they are known. It's not like a coin that has a mintage of 100,000 but there are only 100 known. Classic coins have real rarity, but the number of manufactured low-mintage coins that are falsely "rare" hurts the hobby. The number of these low-mintage issues that have been created in the last five years has been numerous, and while they were hot for several years, I fear that we've flooded the market with false rarity. Far more interesting is a lottery-style purchase opportunity with the US Mint or something that is produced for and released into circulation that creates a "treasure hunt."

Brian Timmons

Modern coins are a driving force in the market and, I believe, are the true "pulse" of the hobby.

Brian Hodge

Well, it brings many new collectors in, and that's tremendously important for all of us. The modern coin market is also its own force, and anytime we can get people involved, it's better for everyone from every walk of the numismatic community.

Jeff Kierstead

There will always be interest in low-mintage coins from certain series, but I think the lure of easy money has caused worldwide Mints to strike way too many coins for collectors over the past two or three decades.

Chris B.

I only endeavor to buy low-mintage planned or instant rarities to complete a series (or as part of a birth-year set for the grandchildren). I am truly jaded by the coin-market-turned-trading-card-market mentality.

James Sibley

The $64,000 question (a phrase fewer and fewer of us may relate to) is whether or not there's someone willing to pay you as much (or hopefully more; remember the time-value of money) as what you paid for that modern, low-mintage coin.

A personal story: When I returned to collecting in December 2018, I thought 1995-W Proof Silver Eagles would be the ticket to riches and bought a couple graded PF 69 Specimens for $3,000 apiece. That was seven years ago. Guess what they're worth today? Yep, $3,000. If I had put that money into a CD...

As premiums above melt have long disintegrated for US Double Eagles, I have noticed that many better and semi-key dates of the series in VF-XF condition can be purchased for very little over melt. Is this a good idea for the collector who can afford to buy a few collectible gold coins? Or are MS-quality coins a better option?

Jim Stoutjesdyk

With the spot price of gold rising so quickly, many $20 gold coins that used to have high premiums over melt now barely trade for a premium at all. I think there is good value for collectors to buy coins like 1883-CC, 1884-CC and 1922-S $20 gold coins in XF condition that can now be had for around 10% over melt value or less.

Jeff Garrett

I love the early better dates and semi-key dates at these levels. They are much rarer than the later-date issues in MS quality. Check the population report for these. There is no comparison. The only problem is that you have to be comfortable buying gold coins with gold above $4,000 per ounce.

Bob Green

MS coins are always a better option. Few better date coins will sell for melt, but the rise in gold has diluted premiums substantially. We love them all VF-67 or finer. Hard to find low-end circulations in any quantity and low-end sets are unlikely to be built.

Chris B.

Yes — a good idea. When the average income catches up with recent inflation the premiums of all grades will expand again. I do not see a demise in the price of metals coming in our collective lifetimes. I like the MS quality because I am not trying to collect "one of each," and I do not want to upgrade as I feel that upgrading is generally a money-losing and time-consuming activity.

Kevin Lipton

If anybody likes gold at these levels, then scarce numismatic gold at small premiums represents a fabulous opportunity. When the numismatic community gets truly comfortable with gold at these new levels, the premiums will grow.

Jeff Kierstead

Many of the circulated better dates are trading for a lower percentage over their gold value (if you can believe the current published prices) than I have ever seen. If the spot prices hold, these rarer dates will start to seem very attractive, and prices could be pushed up.

John Brush

I think that there are some good values in the really low-mintage dates in the $20 Liberty series, but with the release of the Fairmont hoard and the occasional hoards that are found in European banks, you have to really rely on mintage numbers and the availability of certain dates. Honestly, I'd rather save my money and buy a coin that's uncirculated that I really like instead of value-playing for low-mintage issues that have low premiums.

Brian Timmons

I believe finding value in $20 gold pieces is a fantastic way to enter into gold investing. Many better-date $20 Saints and Libs can be purchased for a nominal premium over the price of gold and offer a buffer if gold were to drop. I could actually see a market where gold retreated to $2,500 and better dates like CC $20's and low-mintage Saints could actually go up — even though the price of gold went down — as the coins from the Fairmont Hoard are just about fully dispersed and many gold rarities from that, that have been easy to find for the last few years, are drying up.

Brian Hodge

Quality will always be king, as a natural diamond in flawless condition will always be. Certain key dates in VF-XF condition can be an excellent option, though.

Generally, what are your customers targeting? Have their collecting habits been altered due to escalating precious metals prices?

Jeff Garrett

Most of my clients love coins with original, fresh surfaces that have good eye appeal. The demand for premium-quality coins is higher than it has ever been. This applies to every series.

Bob Green

I've spent my career guiding clients into set building, and I continue to do so. It's what makes collecting fun and enjoyable while building value along the way. Pride of ownership is one of the greatest rewards a collector has.

Kevin Lipton

No real change. We sell coins for a few hundred each to millions of dollars. I just sold a Unique Birch Cent for almost $2 million and the 1794 $1 in 66+ for almost $10 million.

John Brush

Our collector base hasn't really adjusted. Rare coins are still our top item and I think that 19th century coins have gained a little respect in the past year.

Brian Timmons

I have savvy customers purchasing rare gold coins that I have described above as there are a ton of "value plays" in pre-’33 gold right now. I do see some collectors trading in gold bullion for coins they have always wanted. I recently placed a Pan-Pac Octagonal $50 with a client who would have never purchased a coin with a six-figure price tag without offsetting the cost with gold bullion that he had purchased years ago.

Brian Hodge

My customers have been targeting very high-end Proof gold and world rarities. My largest customers are unaffected by precious metals prices, but I believe that it has absolutely affected the rest of the market. It's sort of priced a lot of people out.

Chris B.

I do not have any customers, but the escalating metals prices have not changed my collecting habits. I figure that I have been buying coins for almost 60 years, so my collecting activities are just dollar cost averaging, in my opinion. I do try to limit myself to high-quality encapsulated coins and paper money from the various countries I have lived in — I'm not trying to get "one of each," just a nice assortment.

Jeff Kierstead

I think many buyers are targeting key dates and better type. That hasn't changed, but I think what has changed is that many people who were buying gold and silver because it was a good value have stopped.

James Sibley

As a collector who learned the value of money when I started delivering papers at age 10, every time I take a leap and bid multiple times on Greysheet or substantially more than CDN, I'm gritting my teeth (and still not winning lots more times than not). The dramatic escalation in prices these last five years for the better coins is, at times, difficult to digest. Is it wise to invest in a particular coin (whether we want to admit it or not, we're all hoping to at least get our money back when we sell our coins), at a price that is so much higher than what the same or similar coin sold for pre-COVID? Am I being lured into a tulip-bulb scenario? That being said, I still love the hobby and adding a new coin to my collection. C'est la vie.

For the professional numismatist or seasoned coindexter, is the business and hobby still enjoyable?

Jim Stoutjesdyk

I am so blessed that I was able to turn the hobby of numismatics that I love so much into a career. Sure, I work super hard and have devoted a large part of my life to Heritage, but the satisfaction I receive from doing my job is immeasurable. I get to see tremendous rarities on an almost daily basis that most people just dream about. I spend millions of dollars each month buying coins (lately it's been over $1 million each week!) and get to be the temporary custodian of all those coins. Most importantly, I've made tremendous friendships, some spanning many decades, and I look forward to coin shows and numismatic events like the ANA Summer Seminar and Witter Coin University when I can spend time with my numismatic "family."

Jeff Kierstead

I think it has always been about the thrill of the hunt and the appreciation of rarity. These days, one is more likely to find himself testing gold or counting silver, but not knowing what you might find at a show or what might walk through the door of your shop always keeps things interesting.

Jeff Garrett

I love being a rare coin dealer and can't wait to go to work every day. You never know what the next phone call will be about. I'm truly blessed and love what I do!

Bob Green

I wake up every day so thrilled to be part of the numismatic industry. It's been nearly 40 years and I still get excited to buy and sell rarities. I guess that dates back to being a collector from a very young age.

Kevin Lipton

Of course!

John Brush

Absolutely. I wouldn't be doing it if it wasn't. It's my career, my hobby and still my passion. I'm blessed to share it with friends in the office every day and with one of my sons as well. One of these days, my wife might appreciate it as well. That's my goal!

Dan Goevert

Yes, it's more enjoyable now than at any point during my 60-year coin collecting journey. As a semi-retired person, I now have more time to devote to the hobby.

Brian Timmons

I love this business and hobby. I find it extremely enjoyable and rewarding, and I don't mean financially. Whether I am helping families and collectors liquidate estates or assisting a client in building an investment quality numismatic portfolio, I take great pride and feel very lucky to be a part of every coin's story!

Brian Hodge

Absolutely it is, and it's what you make of it. We're venturing into a new world now of social media, and just look at what that's done to the card industry and so on. We're a little behind in that regard, but we're slowly creeping up.

Chris B.

Yes! I love history and I enjoy the thrill of the search and capture.

James Sibley

Absolutely! As has been said by many, the joy is in the hunt. While I seem to be landing fewer coins these days, I enjoy no less viewing online auction catalogs and dealer websites in the off-chance I'll find that one coin that I've been looking for since forever.


Well, that's a wrap for number 15! Again, I want to thank all of the contributors for their insights and their time for this annual exercise; I truly appreciate it. What magic and storylines will lead the narrative for number 16? Who knows, but I can hardly wait!

Until next time, be safe and happy collecting!
