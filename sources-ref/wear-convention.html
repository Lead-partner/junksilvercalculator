How to Sell Silver Coins for Melt Value

If you own silver coins, you might not realize that you have options beyond selling them as they are. One of these options is selling your silver coins for their melt value.

Melt value allows you to sell purely for the silver content of the coins, rather than their brand or historical reputation. However, it’s important to weigh this strategy against selling coins with numismatic or collectible value.

Let’s explore why, how, and where you might sell your silver coins for their melt value.

What is Silver Melt Value?

Silver melt value refers to the value of your silver if it were melted down into pure silver bullion.

The actual dollar amount is determined by multiplying the mass of a coin’s silver content by the spot price of silver. The spot price represents the current trading value of a troy ounce of silver. To calculate melt value, you multiply the amount of silver in troy ounces by the spot price.

It’s crucial to understand that melt value differs from the market value of coins. Melt value represents the base worth of a coin. However, silver coins—especially rare or collectible ones—may be valued far above their melt value due to rarity, popularity, or historical significance.

Every silver investor should understand the melt value of their coins. However, the goals of a melt value investor are quite different from those of a numismatist or collector.

If you believe your silver coins lack significant value beyond their silver content, selling them for melt value may be a quicker way to liquidate them. Even if a small premium applies to the coin, chasing additional value might not be worthwhile.

Factors That Influence Silver Coin Melt Value

Several factors contribute to a silver coin’s melt value. Because melt value is a dynamic figure, it’s essential to understand what drives its changes.

The most important factor is the spot price of silver. Technically, this is the trading price for the shortest-term futures contracts of silver, but it effectively reflects the current market price of a troy ounce of silver.

Another factor is the silver content of your coin. Some coins, such as Canadian Maple Leafs, are nearly pure silver, while most coins are silver alloys. For instance, a 1-ounce coin with 90% purity contains 0.9 troy ounces of silver, so its melt value is the spot price multiplied by 0.9.

Most US silver coins are not one-ounce coins, though, which is where people go wrong. Here is what the common 90% junk silver coins actually contain:

  • Dime (1946-1964) – 0.0723 troy oz
  • Quarter (1932-1964) – 0.1808 troy oz
  • Half dollar (through 1964) – 0.3617 troy oz
  • Half dollar (1965-1970, 40% silver) – 0.1479 troy oz
  • Morgan or Peace dollar – 0.7734 troy oz

The trade shorthand is that $1 of face value in 90% coin holds about 0.715 troy ounces of silver, which is the number dealers quote from. Note the 1965-1970 half dollars: they look like the earlier ones but hold less than half the silver.

Although collectible value does not affect melt value, it cannot be ignored. For example, a 90% silver coin produced by the United States holds value based purely on its silver content. However, such coins are often historic artifacts. Melting them down would not only destroy their cultural significance but could also be a poor financial choice.

The condition of the coin may influence its overall value but only impacts its melt value if damage reduces the silver content. Less silver means a lower melt value.

How to Calculate the Melt Value of Your Silver Coins

To calculate the melt value of your coins, start by confirming their silver content. While coins are typically struck with a specific composition, it’s critical to authenticate your coins to ensure they are genuine. Counterfeit coins have no true melt value, and excessive wear might reduce the silver content.

  1. Determine the purity of your coin and calculate the total amount of pure silver it contains. Multiply the coin’s weight (preferably in troy ounces) by its purity percentage.
  2. Multiply this amount by the current spot price of silver to determine the melt value.

Keep in mind that professional buyers rarely offer the full melt value. They must account for processing and profit, meaning they will likely deduct a premium from the calculated value.

Fortunately, there are melt value calculators and apps available to simplify these calculations.

It’s also critical to monitor the spot price of silver closely. We provide a real-time feed of silver prices on all our pages, including our homepage, to help you stay informed. Silver prices can fluctuate significantly in short periods.

Where to Sell Silver Coins for Melt Value

Before you sell, it is worth reading how to value silver properly so you know what a fair offer looks like. Now that you understand how to calculate melt value, the next question is where to sell your coins. Here are some common options:

  • Online bullion dealers: Dealers like JM Bullion often offer competitive prices due to their industry competition. This benefits you as the customer.
  • Local coin and precious metal shops: Depending on your location, you may find several shops willing to buy coins based on melt value.
  • Precious metals exchanges: These platforms may accept coins for melt value, particularly if they are open to trading scrap metal.
  • Online auction platforms: Websites like eBay allow you to sell coins directly to buyers, though most will only pay for melt value unless the coins have unique qualities.
  • Private buyers and investors: You can find these buyers through local shops or social media. While less common, this option is worth exploring.

How to Sell Silver Coins for Melt Value

When selling silver coins for melt value, it’s important to approach the process carefully:

  1. Research multiple buyers: Shop around for offers to ensure you’re getting the best deal. Be cautious of offers that seem too good to be true.
  2. Understand payment methods: Some buyers may pay via check, wire transfer, or electronic wallets like PayPal or Venmo. Ensure the method works for you.
  3. Prepare your coins: Do not clean them. Cleaning does nothing for melt value – a refiner is buying the metal, not the surface – and if it turns out a coin was worth more than melt, cleaning will have destroyed that premium permanently. Have anything you are unsure about looked at before it goes in the pile.
  4. Package and ship securely: Use reliable packaging and consider insurance if shipping coins. This protects your investment during transit.
  5. Check tax implications: Any profit from the sale of silver coins is subject to capital gains tax, and the IRS treats precious metals as collectibles, which carries its own rate. Budget accordingly, and check the current rules rather than relying on a general figure.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid When Selling Silver Coins for Melt Value

  • Avoid dealing with untrustworthy buyers. If something feels off, walk away.
  • Stay informed about the spot price of silver. Understanding long-term price trends can help you avoid selling during a market dip.
  • Be aware of transaction fees, which can reduce your overall profit. Look out for terms like “processing fee” or “administrative fee” and evaluate if the deal is still worthwhile.

Conclusion

Knowing the melt value of your silver coins is crucial, whether you intend to sell them for their silver content or their collectible value. Melt value is calculated by multiplying the amount of pure silver in your coins by the spot price of silver, making it essential to monitor the market before selling.
